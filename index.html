A research-backed guide for parents and future parents

Your family may already have a language policy. Is it the one you want?

Every household makes choices about language — who speaks what, to whom, and when — whether or not anyone has written them down. This guide helps you see those choices clearly and shape them on purpose, using what research on bilingual childhoods actually shows. The three strands you see here — beliefs, practices, and management — run through the whole site.

The idea in one minute

What is Family Language Policy?

Researchers call it Family Language Policy (FLP): the explicit and overt planning families do about language use at home, along with the everyday habits that end up shaping which languages a child hears, uses, and keeps.King et al., 2008 The field borrows a three-part model from language policy research: every policy — whether a country's or a kitchen table's — has beliefs, practices, and management.Spolsky, 2012 Select a card to see each part in a real household.

Beliefs: the "why" underneath everything

Beliefs are the ideas a family holds about what languages are for and worth. Does Spanish mean "family," "school," "the past," or "opportunity"? Is a child who answers in English "losing" something, or doing something normal? Researchers find that these ideologies shape day-to-day language practices and the plans families make.King et al., 2008

Beliefs often go unexamined. Bilingualism researcher Annick De Houwer notes that for some couples raising a child with two languages is a conscious decision, while for many others it is simply "a matter of course and not a matter of choice."De Houwer, 2009, p. 4 Either way, one belief matters so much it has a name: the impact belief, the sense that what parents do with language actually affects what the child will do. Parents who hold it tend to act on it; parents who assume "it will just happen" (or "it is hopeless") tend not to.De Houwer, 1999 And beliefs carry emotional weight: when the result is not what parents hoped, "the guilt can be overwhelming."De Houwer, 2009, p. 71

Beliefs also travel: much of the world grows up multilingual as a matter of course, yet many Western, middle-class contexts treat one language as the default.Ortega, 2013, pp. 3–5 Noticing which assumptions you absorbed is step one.

Example · The Okafor–Lindqvist household
Dad grew up speaking Igbo and English; Mum grew up with Swedish. They live in Ohio. Unspoken belief: "English will take care of itself; the other two are a gift we have to give on purpose." Once they said it out loud, their Sunday calls to Lagos and Göteborg stopped being optional.
Try asking
"If our child is fifteen and speaks only English, what will we wish we had done?" The answer is your belief, in plain words.

Practices: what really happens between 6 and 8 p.m.

Practices are the languages people actually use, to whom, about what. They are the part of a policy you can observe without asking anyone. Researchers measure them because they are what the child experiences: how much of each language, from how many people, in which kinds of conversation.

Practices matter because exposure matters. In studies of young Spanish–English bilingual toddlers, how far along children were in each language tracked the relative amount of that language they heard, and the number of different speakers and the share of input from fluent speakers added something extra.Hoff et al., 2012Place & Hoff, 2011

Practices also drift. In one large survey of bilingual parents, over 90% reported regularly mixing their languages within a sentence when talking with their child — even though most research had only studied families who kept languages strictly separate.Byers-Heinlein, 2013 Real homes are messier than textbook strategies, and that is normal.

Example · The Okafor–Lindqvist household, observed
Breakfast: English (everyone is rushing). Daycare: English. Dinner: mostly English, Swedish from Mum when she remembers. Bedtime: Swedish songs. Igbo: only on calls, and only when Grandma leads. Their stated plan said "both heritage languages every day." Their practices said "Swedish at bedtime, Igbo on Sundays."
Try this
For three ordinary days, jot down which language each routine actually happened in. No judgement; just data.

Management: the deliberate part

Management is anything a family does on purpose to influence practices: deciding who speaks what, protecting a routine for a language, choosing a bilingual preschool, booking video calls with grandparents, filling a shelf with books in the target language, or asking a caregiver to use it.

Management includes well-known strategies such as "one parent, one language" (OPOL), but strategies are tools, not guarantees. In a questionnaire study of 1,899 families in Flanders, all the children spoke the majority language, but not all of them spoke the minority language (the one less used in the wider community) — and the OPOL pattern turned out to be neither necessary nor sufficient for a child to use it. Homes where both parents used the minority language, and at most one also used the majority language, had the best odds.De Houwer, 2007

Management also responds to the child. Children negotiate too — answering in the community language, asking a parent to "talk normal," or suddenly wanting to impress a cousin.King et al., 2008 A good plan expects this and adapts rather than treating every switch as failure.

Example · The Okafor–Lindqvist household, managed
They kept what already worked (Swedish bedtime) and added one protected Igbo routine: Saturday pancake-making with a Lagos video call propped on the counter. Mum, who speaks no Igbo, agreed that being left out of twenty minutes a week was fine. Three months later they will check what stuck.
Rule of thumb
Manage the routines you can protect, not the whole day. One anchored routine beats a rule nobody keeps.

Raising a bilingual or multilingual child is not about finding one perfect rule. It is about making an intentional, flexible family language plan that fits your goals, relationships, routines, resources, and community.

The stakes

Why planning on purpose matters

Not because bilingualism is a race or a brain upgrade, but because a language is a set of relationships. Research on family language policy argues that the choices families make shape children's developmental paths, connect with how they do at school, and — added up across many homes — decide whether minority languages survive at all.King et al., 2008

Connection across generations

When a child and a grandparent share no comfortable language, the relationship runs through translators. Family connection and heritage pride are among the personal benefits of knowing more than one language that our course highlighted.Montee, 2026, slide 26

Keeping a heritage language alive

Children exposed to two languages reliably learn the community language; the heritage language — the one less widely used in your community — is the one that needs protecting. In a study of nearly 1,900 bilingual-input families, every child spoke the majority language, but not every child spoke the minority oneDe Houwer, 2007 — roughly one family in four where children hear two languages ends up with a child who speaks only one.De Houwer, 2009, p. 8

Identity and belonging

Family beliefs about language shape how children come to see themselves and where they belong.King et al., 2008 SLA researchers distinguish a heritage context — a language tied to family and community rather than school or travel — precisely because the relationship to that language is different.Ortega, 2013, p. 6

Access to relationships and cultural life

Jokes, recipes, songs, religious services, group chats with cousins: a second language is a door to all of them. Researchers describe the ideal as harmonious bilingual development — families experiencing well-being with their languages, not stress about them.De Houwer, 2015

Closing the hope–habit gap

Families often hope for more than their routines deliver. Research suggests strict language separation is rarer in real homes than in research samples — over 90% of parents in one broad survey mixed languages regularly — so plans built on ideal days quietly fail on ordinary ones.Byers-Heinlein, 2013

What this guide deliberately does not promise

You may have read that bilingual children get smarter, do better at school, or gain a lifelong cognitive edge. Some studies report advantages on specific attention tasks, and our course discussed these as "in some ways" true,Montee, 2026, slide 17 but the evidence is debated and depends heavily on context. This guide does not rest its case on it. The reasons above — relationships, identity, heritage, and well-being — stand on their own.

Myth versus reality

Five things parents are told that research complicates

Each "reality" below is nuanced on purpose. Where the research is mixed or depends on context, it says so.

Reality

Growing up with two or more languages is the majority human experience, not an exotic risk.Ortega, 2013, p. 4 Children learning two languages from birth hit the big milestones (babbling, first words, two-word combinations, sentences) on the same rough timetable as children learning one, and the wide variation in when is the same normal variation monolingual children show.De Houwer, 2009, pp. 5–6 Our course textbook is blunt about it: there is "no evidence for the pervasive myth that young bilingual children are confused and cannot get their languages straight."De Houwer, 2009, p. 72

What does happen: young bilingual children typically know fewer words in each language than a monolingual peer does in their one language, because their time is split — but when you count words across both languages, totals are comparable.Hoff et al., 2012Montee, 2026, slide 13 That is arithmetic, not confusion.

One honest caveat: in a large sample of bilingual families, more parental within-sentence language mixing was linked to somewhat smaller comprehension vocabularies at 1.5 years, after accounting for how much of the language the child heard. The effect was modest, the author's interpretation was that mixing may obscure cues infants use to sort their languages, and most parents mixed at least sometimes.Byers-Heinlein, 2013 Don't panic about mixing — but speaking in whole sentences of one language, when you can, is reasonable: the less mixing children hear, the less they tend to produce.De Houwer, 2009, p. 63

Try: When your toddler points at the dog and you say the word in both languages, finish the sentence in one of them before switching.

Reality

OPOL is one strategy among several, and research does not crown it. In De Houwer's survey of 1,899 families with children aged 6–10, the OPOL input pattern "did not provide a necessary nor sufficient input condition" for children to speak the minority (heritage) language. Patterns in which both parents used that language at home, with at most one also using the majority language, had a high chance of success.De Houwer, 2007

Pure OPOL is also rarer than it sounds. Even in households that describe themselves as "one person, one language," children usually hear at least one adult switch between the languages, so the textbook treats strict OPOL as "an idealization."De Houwer, 2009, p. 12 Reviews of family language policy likewise treat OPOL as one policy whose link to outcomes is worth studying, not as a rule,King et al., 2008 and families describe a whole range of real-world strategies, from strict OPOL to freely mixing.Byers-Heinlein, 2013

The underlying principle is simpler than any acronym: the heritage language needs enough exposure from enough people, protected by routines you can keep. OPOL is one way to get there. "Heritage language at home," "a language per place," or "a language per routine" are others.

Try: Instead of asking "which strategy?", ask "where in our week does the heritage language reliably happen, and who else could add to it?"

Reality

This pattern is so common it has a name. Researchers call it a dilingual conversation: a two-language conversation in which one person speaks one language and the other answers in the other. It works because both people understand both.De Houwer, 2009, pp. 8–9 The child is understanding — which is real, measurable language knowledge — and making a social choice about speaking. Children are active participants who negotiate the family's policy, not passive recipients of it.King et al., 2008

It is still worth gently steering, because De Houwer's "harmonious bilingual development" includes children actively using both languages, and one-sided conversations can wear parents down.De Houwer, 2015 The textbook also warns that if these two-language conversations become a habit, children may gradually lose their speaking ability in the heritage language even while they keep understanding it.De Houwer, 2009, p. 64 Parents in research use gentle nudges — asking "What?" as if they had not understood, or repeating the child's answer in the target language — to invite the child to switch. (The technical name is monolingual discourse strategies.)De Houwer, 2009, p. 9 Use them lightly; nobody wants a quiz at dinner.

Also remember that language use shifts across contexts and over time. A six-year-old who answers in English may be a fourteen-year-old who wants to text cousins in Russian. Keep the door open.

Try: Repeat what your child said, once, in the target language — a "recast" — warmly, then carry on: "Ah, ты хочешь сок? Here is your juice." The card below the myths spells this out.

Reality

The "perfect speaker" standard comes from a monolingual bias that SLA researchers have spent two decades unlearning: the habit of treating a monolingual native speaker as the only legitimate benchmark.Ortega, 2013, pp. 5–6 Media stories about effortless "polyglots" make it worse — they trivialize the real time and effort languages take and leave ordinary people feeling that without special talent they should not even try.Pavlenko, 2015

Here is the honest nuance. Input quality does matter: in one study of 2-year-olds, the share of a child's input that came from fluent (native) speakers, and the number of different people they heard the language from, each predicted skill in that language.Place & Hoff, 2011 So a parent with limited fluency should not be the only source — but they can absolutely be a source, especially for songs, routines, and warmth, while relatives, community, books, and media widen the circle.

Remember too that children are not copying you word for word. They actively build their own grammar from the language around them, which is why even monolingual children say "holded" after hearing "held."Gass et al., 2020, pp. 105–106 Your imperfect sentences are raw material, not a mold.

Try: Pick one routine you can do in the language at your level — a bedtime song, counting stairs, naming food — and let fluent speakers own the richer conversation.

Reality

Researchers describe family language policy as something negotiated among family members, not fixed once and filed away.King et al., 2008 Children start school, grandparents move in or pass away, a parent changes jobs, a sibling arrives. Each changes who speaks what to whom.

Even the question of when is less rigid than parents fear. The boundary between learning two languages "at once" and learning one "after" the other is not clear-cut,Gass et al., 2020, pp. 111–112 and "younger is always better" is an oversimplification: younger learners tend to end up with better pronunciation, while older children and adults can learn many aspects of language quickly and well.Gass et al., 2020, pp. 112–113Montee, 2026, slide 12 A plan that starts later, or restarts after a lull, is still a plan.

Treat your plan like a family budget: written down, revisited at natural turning points, and adjusted without guilt.

Try: Put a "language check-in" on the calendar for the first week of every school term. Ten minutes. What is working, what has drifted, what is one thing to change?

What do I say in the moment?

When your child understands you but answers in English

A plain-English script. The exchange is shown in translation; you would say your lines in the target language.

You, in the target language"Do you want juice?"

Child, in English"Yes! Juice!"

You, in the target language"Ah, you want juice. Here is your juice. Apple or orange?"

Child, in English"Apple."

You, in the target language"Apple it is. Can you hold the cup with two hands?"

And the conversation moves on. No quiz, no standoff.

  • Don't turn it into a test. Your child understood you; that is real language knowledge. Family well-being around the languages matters as much as output.De Houwer, 2015
  • Respond in the target language, briefly repeating their point. That is the gentle nudge parents in research use. Asking "What?" as if you had not understood is the firmer version; save it for occasional use.De Houwer, 2009, p. 9
  • Keep the conversation meaningful and moving. Children build language from real back-and-forth, not from corrections.Gass et al., 2020, pp. 105–106 The reason to keep nudging: if answering in English becomes the household habit, speaking skill in the heritage language can fade even while understanding stays.De Houwer, 2009, p. 64

Normal variation is wide

Children growing up bilingual vary a great deal from one another in how, and how much, they encounter each language, and that variety is the norm rather than a problem.Byers-Heinlein, 2013 How much a child says, in which language, with whom, can change from one context to another and from one year to the next. The main factors behind differences between bilingual children are how often they hear each language, how that changes over time, how much mixed speech they hear, and whether adults use monolingual or bilingual discourse strategies with them.De Houwer, 2009, p. 70 If you are worried about your child's overall communication — in all their languages together — talk to a pediatrician or speech-language professional who has experience with bilingual children. Be wary of anyone whose first suggestion is to drop a language: the textbook calls that advice "simply unethical" when it rests on the assumption that bilingual input is the problem.De Houwer, 2009, p. 7

Three composite families

What this looks like in real life

These are composite scenarios written for teaching. They combine patterns described in the research with everyday situations; they are not real research participants or real families.

Д

Dasha and Mike, and two-year-old Lev

Dasha grew up in Novosibirsk; Mike speaks only English. Dasha's parents are in Russia and video-call weekly. They live in Maryland. Dasha's goal: Lev should be able to talk with his grandparents, not just wave at the screen.

1 The challenge

  • Dasha is Lev's only in-person Russian source, and she switches to English whenever Mike is in the room, which is most evenings.
  • Lev already answers Dasha in English half the time (the two-language pattern researchers call dilingual).De Houwer, 2009, pp. 8–9
  • Research links skill in a language to how much of it a child hears and from how many peopleHoff et al., 2012Place & Hoff, 2011 — right now Russian is one voice, part-time.

2 A realistic starting plan

  • Belief, said aloud: "Russian is how Lev will know Babushka and Dedushka."
  • Practice: Dasha stays in Russian with Lev even when Mike is present; Mike learns the twenty household phrases so he feels included.
  • Management: Russian owns bath-and-bedtime (every night) and Saturday-morning cooking (with grandparents on a call, giving instructions). A Russian playgroup once a month adds new voices.
  • When Lev answers in English, Dasha repeats it once in Russian and moves on — no standoffs.

3 How it may need to adapt

  • At preschool entry English input will jump. The plan should expect Russian output to dip and respond by adding exposure (a Russian-speaking babysitter, longer calls), not by pressure.
  • If grandparents visit for a month, that is a natural immersion window: let them lead and let Dasha rest.
  • By school age, Dasha may want to add reading; a Saturday school becomes the question to revisit.
서

Seo-yeon and Mateo, and four-year-old Luna

Seo-yeon's first language is Korean, Mateo's is Spanish; they speak English with each other. They live in a small English-dominant town with no Korean or Spanish community nearby. They want Luna to have both, and they are exhausted.

1 The challenge

  • Three languages compete for a limited number of waking hours; English wins by default because it is the couple's shared language and the town's.
  • They tried strict OPOL and felt like failures when it slipped. But OPOL is neither necessary nor sufficient,De Houwer, 2007 and the stress itself is a problem: well-being is part of healthy bilingual development.De Houwer, 2015
  • Luna has a smaller vocabulary in each language than her monolingual classmates — expected when words are split across languages — and a relative made a comment.Hoff et al., 2012

2 A realistic starting plan

  • Belief, renegotiated: "Both languages, both imperfectly, over many years" replaces "both languages, perfectly, now."
  • Practice: Each parent uses their language in one-to-one time with Luna; family time together stays English without guilt.
  • Management: Spanish anchors the morning routine and the drive to preschool (Mateo's shift). Korean anchors dinner prep and bedtime books (Seo-yeon's shift). Sunday video calls alternate between grandparents.
  • They order ten picture books per language and put the English ones away for a month.

3 How it may need to adapt

  • If one language lags badly, they may choose to prioritize it for a season rather than split evenly — plans are negotiated, not fixed.King et al., 2008
  • If a Spanish-speaking family moves to town, that friendship is worth more than any app: new speakers add something exposure hours alone do not.Place & Hoff, 2011
  • At kindergarten, they revisit whether a dual-language program or a summer with grandparents is realistic.
J

Jun, 29, planning a family

Jun's parents emigrated from the Philippines; Jun understands Tagalog well but speaks it haltingly and never learned to read it. Jun's partner does not speak it. They are not parents yet but want their future children to have the language Jun nearly lost.

1 The challenge

  • Jun is a heritage speaker — a context SLA researchers treat as its own category, tied to family and community rather than school or travel.Ortega, 2013, p. 6
  • Jun feels unqualified. That standard comes from a monolingual bias in how we judge speakersOrtega, 2013, pp. 5–6 and from "polyglot" media that make real language learning look either effortless or hopeless.Pavlenko, 2015
  • Still, input from fluent speakers and a range of speakers does help children,Place & Hoff, 2011 so Jun alone will not be enough.

2 A realistic starting plan

  • Belief, written down now: "Our kids will hear Tagalog from people who love them, including me, imperfectly."
  • Practice, starting before the baby: Jun switches to Tagalog with their own parents for one call a week and starts a conversation class — rebuilding comfort, not perfection.
  • Management, pre-planned: Lola and Lolo (Jun's parents) are asked, explicitly, to be the Tagalog-only grandparents. Jun collects songs, board books, and lullabies now.
  • Jun's partner agrees on what "being left out" of Tagalog moments will feel like and how they will handle it.

3 How it may need to adapt

  • When the baby arrives, Jun's own Tagalog may stall from exhaustion. The plan leans on grandparents and songs, then rebuilds.
  • If a grandparent's health changes, the "Tagalog-only grandparents" plan needs a replacement source: a Filipino community group or caregiver.
  • As the child grows, Jun may learn to read Tagalog alongside them — a shared project rather than a parent-expert dynamic.

Composite scenarios are illustrative. Names, places, and details are invented; research citations point to the findings each scenario draws on, not to studies of these families.

Low pressure, high leverage

Start this week

You do not need a perfect plan to begin. Tick these off as you go; the checkmarks live only on this page.

  1. Write down your family's "why"

    One or two sentences. Beliefs drive practices,King et al., 2008 and a why you can read on a hard day is worth more than any strategy name.

  2. Pick one recurring routine for each language

    Bedtime, breakfast, the drive — something that already happens. Anchoring a language to a routine protects exposure, which is what children's skill in each language tracks.Hoff et al., 2012

  3. Make interaction meaningful, not performative

    Talk about things — the dog, the soup, the plan for Saturday — rather than drilling words. Children build language actively from the conversation around them, not by imitation.Gass et al., 2020, pp. 105–106 For young children, in-person interaction is what counts; shows and apps are a supplement, not a substitute.Montee, 2026, slide 16

  4. Widen the circle

    Relatives, caregivers, friends, books, music, community events. The number of different people a child hears a language from, and the share of fluent speakers among them, each add something.Place & Hoff, 2011 One new voice this month is a win.

  5. Notice what your child responds to

    Children help set the policy whether you like it or not.King et al., 2008 If songs land and flashcards flop, do more songs. If they answer in the other language, recast once and keep going.

  6. Schedule a check-in, and drop the guilt

    Put a ten-minute "language check-in" on the calendar for the start of each school term. The goal is a family that feels good about its languages — what researchers call harmonious bilingual development — not a perfect record.De Houwer, 2015 Many parents raise bilingual children "without a lot of extra work or feelings of inadequacy," and the ones who struggle usually lack support, not commitment.De Houwer, 2009, p. 71

If you only do one thing

Protect one routine in the target language, every day, with someone the child loves. Everything else can be added later. What children need, in the textbook's closing words, is "a warm, nurturing environment in which children are free to learn, and plenty of opportunities to hear and use whatever language or languages they are learning."De Houwer, 2009, p. 73

Used for: raising a child bilingually being a conscious decision for some couples and "a matter of course" for others (p. 4); the milestones box and the point that the wide variation in timing is the same normal variation seen in monolingual children (pp. 5–6); the warning that advising parents to drop a language is "simply unethical" when it assumes bilingual input is the problem (p. 7); the finding that in "every fourth family" where children hear two languages they speak only one (p. 8); dilingual conversations and monolingual discourse strategies (pp. 8–9); strict one-person-one-language as "an idealization" (p. 12); mixed utterances declining when children hear fewer of them (p. 63); dilingual conversations becoming a habit that erodes speaking ability (p. 64); the main factors behind variation between bilingual children (p. 70); "parental work" and guilt (p. 71); the absence of evidence that bilingual children are confused (p. 72); and the closing description of what children need (p. 73).

Core outside research: five peer-reviewed articles found independently

Peer-reviewed review

King, K. A., Fogle, L., & Logan-Terry, A. (2008). Family language policy. Language and Linguistics Compass, 2(5), 907–922. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-818X.2008.00076.x

How we used it

The field-defining review. Provides the definition of family language policy as "explicit and overt planning in relation to language use within the home among family members"; frames FLP as bringing together language policy and child language acquisition research; reports that language ideologies shape family language practices; treats "one person–one language" as one policy whose link to child outcomes is studied rather than assumed; describes language as managed, learned, and negotiated within families; and argues that FLPs shape children's developmental trajectories, connect with school success, and collectively determine the maintenance of minority languages.

Peer-reviewed study

De Houwer, A. (2007). Parental language input patterns and children's bilingual use. Applied Psycholinguistics, 28(3), 411–424. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716407070221

How we used it

Questionnaire study of 1,899 families in Flanders with a child aged 6–10 and at least one parent speaking a language other than Dutch. All children spoke the majority language, but minority-language use was not universal; parental input patterns correlated with child minority-language use; patterns in which both parents used the minority language and at most one also used the majority language had a high chance of success; and the one parent–one language pattern was "neither necessary nor sufficient." Used for the OPOL myth, the "heritage language needs protecting" point, and the plan builder's exposure notes. Only claims stated in the published abstract are used; specific percentages are deliberately not quoted.

Peer-reviewed article

De Houwer, A. (2015). Harmonious bilingual development: Young families' well-being in language contact situations. International Journal of Bilingualism, 19(2), 169–184. https://doi.org/10.1177/1367006913489202

How we used it

Defines harmonious bilingual development as the experience of well-being in a language-contact situation involving young children and their families; proposes as its elements parent–child conversations largely in a single language, children's active use of both languages, and roughly comparable proficiency; and identifies positive attitudes to early bilingualism, discourse socialization patterns, and how often children hear each language as likely contributing factors. Used to center family well-being, to support gently steering dilingual conversations, and for the "drop the guilt" step.

Peer-reviewed study

Hoff, E., Core, C., Place, S., Rumiche, R., Señor, M., & Parra, M. (2012). Dual language exposure and early bilingual development. Journal of Child Language, 39(1), 1–27. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305000910000759

How we used it

Compared bilingually and monolingually developing children aged 1;10 to 2;6 from similar high-SES backgrounds. Monolingual children were ahead on single-language measures of vocabulary and grammar, but the groups were comparable on total vocabulary; within the bilingual group, every vocabulary and grammar measure tracked the relative amount of input in that language. Used for the "confusion" myth and every claim that exposure in a language drives skill in that language.

Peer-reviewed study

Byers-Heinlein, K. (2013). Parental language mixing: Its measurement and the relation of mixed input to young bilingual children's vocabulary size. Bilingualism: Language and Cognition, 16(1), 32–48. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1366728912000120

How we used it

181 bilingual parents completed a new Language Mixing Scale; over 90% reported regularly mixing languages within sentences when speaking to their child. Higher mixing predicted significantly smaller comprehension vocabularies at 1.5 years and marginally smaller production vocabularies at 2 years after controlling for exposure, age, gender, and balance; amount of exposure was the strongest predictor overall. The article also notes that bilingual children vary considerably in how they encounter their languages, that families use strategies ranging from OPOL to free mixing, and that earlier research mostly sampled families who avoided mixing. Used for the mixing caveat, the "hope–habit gap," and the normal-variation note.

Supporting works (framework attribution or a single detail)

Framework source

Spolsky, B. (2012). Family language policy – the critical domain. Journal of Multilingual and Multicultural Development, 33(1), 3–11. https://doi.org/10.1080/01434632.2011.638072

How we used it

Source of the three-component model of language policy — practices, beliefs (ideology), and management — applied to the family domain. Used only for attributing the framework.

Peer-reviewed study

Place, S., & Hoff, E. (2011). Properties of dual language exposure that influence 2-year-olds' bilingual proficiency. Child Development, 82(6), 1834–1849. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2011.01660.x

How we used it

Diary study of 29 Spanish–English bilingual 25-month-olds. Relative exposure predicted outcomes in each language; the number of different speakers and the percentage of input from native speakers were unique predictors of English skills. Used for "widen the circle" and the nuance in the "perfect speaker" myth.

Related work by a course author

De Houwer, A. (1999). Environmental factors in early bilingual development: The role of parental beliefs and attitudes. In G. Extra & L. Verhoeven (Eds.), Bilingualism and migration (pp. 75–96). Mouton de Gruyter. https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110807820.75

How we used it

Source of the term impact belief: a parent's belief that their own language behavior affects their child's language development. Cited once, in the "Beliefs" card. The 2009 course textbook excerpts we read do not use the term, so the original chapter is cited instead.

Educational guide, not individual advice

This site was created as a final project for LING 2050 (How Languages Are Learned) at Georgetown University. It is an educational summary of published research for a general audience. It is not individualized medical, speech-language, developmental, or educational advice. If you have concerns about your child's communication in any or all of their languages, consult a pediatrician or a speech-language pathologist experienced with multilingual children.
